Logical gates

Jan 18, 20264 min read

A logical gate is a unitary operator LLL that maps the codespace C\mathcal{C}C into itself, i.e. such that L∣ψ⟩∈CL \vert \psi \rangle \in \mathcal{C}L∣ψ⟩∈C for all ∣ψ⟩∈C\vert \psi \rangle \in \mathcal{C}∣ψ⟩∈C.
In the context of stabilizer codes, with stabilizer group S\mathcal{S}S, logical gates include stabilizers, but also operators that map one part of the codespace to another part.
They can also be thought as elements of the Normalizer of SSS.

Logical gates

A unitary LLL is a logical gate if and only if it maps the stabilizer group S\mathcal{S}S to itself, i.e. L†SL∈SL^{\dagger} SL \in \mathcal{S}L†SL∈S for all S∈SS \in \mathcal{S}S∈S.
Logical gates form a group.

Using this condition, it is possible to check whether operators are logical gates.

without requiring that L†SL∈SL^{\dagger} SL\in\mathcal{S}L†SL∈S. We deal with such logical gates e.g. in Transversal Gates, where the gates implemented can be non-Pauli.
We will denote this extended set of non-Pauli stabilizers with S~\tilde{\mathcal{S}}S~.

L†SLL^{\dagger}SLL†SL is not a Pauli, it is often more involved to show that it’s a stabilizer. The most general method, that you find in many papers, is to explicitly write the states of the codespace and show that it stabilizes them. One possible alternative consists in showing that L†SLL^{\dagger}SLL†SL is itself a logical gate, acting as the identity (i.e. mapping XLX_LXL​ to XLX_LXL​ and ZLZ_LZL​ to ZLZ_LZL​).

To show that L†SLL^{\dagger}SLL†SL is a logical gate, you either show that it maps Pauli stabilizers to Pauli stabilizers, or you recursively continue this process until the operator maps Pauli stabilizers to Pauli stabilizers.

Whenever we introduce a new potential logical gate for a given code, the first step is always to verify that it maps Pauli stabilizers to extended stabilizers. The second step is to check what logical operation it performs. To determine the effect of a logical gate, there are two possibilities:

  1. We check its effect on logical states. For instance, if we can prove that it maps ∣0⟩L\vert 0\rangle_L∣0⟩L​ to ∣+⟩L\vert +\rangle_L∣+⟩L​, and ∣1⟩L\vert 1\rangle_L∣1⟩L​ to ∣−⟩L\vert -\rangle_L∣−⟩L​, it means we are dealing with a logical Hadamard.
  2. We check its effect on logical Pauli’s. For instance, showing that UXLU†=ZLUX_LU^{\dagger}=Z_LUXL​U†=ZL​ and UZLU†=XLUZ_LU^{\dagger}=X_LUZL​U†=XL​ also proves that UUU is a logical Hadamard.

Pauli logicals

An important family of logical gates are the Pauli logical operators. As expected, those are the logical gates that belong to the Pauli group. As two given Pauli operators can either commute or anticommute, Pauli logicals could either commute or anticommute with stabilizers. However, if a Pauli logical anticommute with a stabilizer, that is SL=−LSSL=-LSSL=−LS, it means that L†SL=−S∉SL^{\dagger} S L = -S \notin \mathcal{S}L†SL=−S∈/S, which contradicts the characterization of logical gates given above. Therefore:

Pauli logicals

A Pauli operator PPP is a logical operator if and only if it commutes with all the stabilizers.

Notably, this means that Pauli logicals form the Centralizer CPn(S){C}_{\mathcal{P}_n}(\mathcal{S})CPn​​(S) of the stabilizer group, which itself is a subgroup of the Pauli group Pn\mathcal{P}_nPn​.

Logical errors

Let’s define non-trivial logical operators (also known as logical errors) as elements of C(S)\S\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}) \backslash \mathcal{S}C(S)\S, or in other words, Pauli operators that commute with all the stabilizers but are not stabilizers themselves.
Note that this means that logical errors send elements of the codespace into different elements of the codespace:

SL∣ψ⟩=LS∣ψ⟩=L∣ψ⟩SL\ket{\psi} = LS\ket{\psi} = L\ket{\psi}SL∣ψ⟩=LS∣ψ⟩=L∣ψ⟩
